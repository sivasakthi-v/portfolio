The Ecosystem

One brand. Three distinct products. One shared system.

Fabrito isn't a single product — it's a platform. The challenge was designing three radically different interfaces for three radically different users, while keeping enough design coherence that they unmistakably belong to the same world.

🏛️

Admin Panel · Web · Desktop

A high-density management interface for the Fabrito operations team — seller onboarding, order monitoring, dispute resolution, analytics, and catalogue approvals. Information density is a feature, not a compromise: administrators live in this dashboard.

📦

Seller App · Mobile · iOS & Android

A mobile-first tool for fabric sellers — most on Android mid-range phones, in market environments. Upload products, manage inventory, track orders, communicate with buyers. Built for one-handed use and intermittent network conditions.

🛍️

B2C Website · Web · Responsive

A consumer-facing storefront for fabric buyers — tailors, designers, small manufacturers. Browse by fabric type, weight, colour, and MOQ. A discovery experience balanced with the technical specifications fabric buyers actually need.

Design Challenges

What made this hard.

Challenge 01

Three users, three mental models

An admin thinks in data tables and exception handling. A seller thinks in inventory and incoming orders. A buyer thinks in fabric types and delivery timelines. Each needed an interface matched to their cognitive model — without fragmenting the underlying system.

Challenge 02

B2B complexity on a mobile screen

Fabric trade involves technical specifics: GSM weight, thread count, dye batch, MOQ, composition percentages. Making this accessible to sellers on a phone — without losing precision or creating overwhelming forms — required serious information-architecture work.

Challenge 03

Trust signals in a low-trust market

The Indian fabric trade has historically run on established, in-person relationships. Digitising it meant designing trust signals that substitute for handshakes — seller verification badges, return-policy clarity, and buyer reviews that feel authentic rather than gamed.

System Thinking

Designed for three users, maintained as one.

Three personas, one empathy map

The Administrator · Fabrito Ops · Desktop-first

Needs high-density data views, quick filtering, exception flagging, and batch operations. Comfort with complexity is an asset — information should be dense but organised, never buried.

Aarav Singh · Fabric Seller · Mobile-first

Operates from a market stall in Surat. Phone in one hand, fabric swatch in the other. Needs to upload a listing in under 2 minutes. Network drops frequently. Every unnecessary tap is a cost.

The Fabric Buyer · Tailor / Designer · Mixed devices

Sourcing fabric for 50 blouses. Needs to filter by composition, weight, and MOQ. Wants to compare across three sellers before committing. Cares about delivery time and minimum-order flexibility.

Shared design principles

Information hierarchy is non-negotiable

Across all three products, the most important information — order status, stock count, price — appears first, largest, and with highest contrast. The user's most critical question is answered before they look for it.

Colour signals state, not decoration

Each colour carries consistent semantic meaning across Admin, Seller App, and Website — so a user who encounters the system across roles orients themselves instantly.

Touch targets are never compromised

The Seller App is designed for one-handed use in a busy market. Every interactive element meets a 48px minimum, and there are no hover-only states — every interaction works with thumb input.

Forms earn their fields

Every field exists because a user decision depends on it. Optional fields are clearly marked. Progressive disclosure keeps initial forms simple and reveals complexity only when needed.

Design Decisions

The logic behind the system.

01

One design system, three surface areas

The most consequential decision was building Fabrito as a design system first, not three separate products. Shared colour tokens, typography scales, and component logic mean a designer working on the Seller App and the Admin Panel uses the same vocabulary. This reduced inconsistency, sped up velocity, and created coherence users can feel even if they can't name it.

02

Status as the primary admin information unit

In the Admin Panel, every key entity — seller, order, product — carries a visible status chip at all times. Making status a first-class UI element (not a buried field) transformed the interface from a database view into an operational tool. Administrators can scan hundreds of rows and find exceptions in seconds.

03

The Seller App is an offline-tolerant experience

Many sellers operate in markets with unreliable connectivity. The app was designed with optimistic UI patterns — actions complete locally and sync when connectivity returns. Draft listings, edited inventory counts, and received order confirmations are cached locally first. The design acknowledges the real environment of use, not an idealised one.

04

Technical specifications at the top of product cards

Fabric buyers are professional purchasers. Their decisions are made on GSM weight, thread count, and composition — not lifestyle photography. The B2C website surfaces these specifications as primary product attributes, with imagery playing a supporting role. Counter to e-commerce convention, but aligned with how fabric buyers actually buy.

Outcomes & Learnings

What the ecosystem delivered.

🏗️ Systems thinking scales

Investing in a shared design system upfront reduced design time on the third product by roughly 40% compared to the first. Decisions in the admin component library directly accelerated website development. System investment is never wasted.

📱 Mobile constraints produce better desktop UX

Designing the Seller App first — with its strict one-handed constraint — produced clearer thinking about task hierarchy that made the Admin Panel better. Solve a problem with the most constraints, and solving it with fewer becomes easier.

🤝 B2B UX is about confidence, not delight

The Admin and Seller App language is deliberately functional over beautiful. B2B users don't want to be charmed — they want to be confident. Every empty state, error message, and confirmation was written to reinforce that confidence.

🌐 Domain knowledge is a design input

Designing for the fabric trade required understanding MOQ structures, GSM measurement, and the informal credit systems underpinning textile trade in India. Without this domain knowledge, several key decisions would have been wrong. Research into the business model shaped the product, not just the user research.
